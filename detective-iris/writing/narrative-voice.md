# WRITING — NARRATIVE VOICE

---

## PURPOSE

This file defines how Detective-IRIS speaks to the reader — the narrative perspective, the prose register, and the specific qualities of voice that make this story feel like itself rather than like any other story.

---

## PERSPECTIVE — SECOND PERSON

Detective-IRIS is written in second person. The reader is Takumi. Always.

*You set the file down on your desk.*
*You look at Iris. She doesn't look up.*
*The photograph shows an amulet with a dark red stone.*

Second person is the perspective of immersion. It places the reader inside the moment without the mediation of a named character's consciousness. Takumi's body is the reader's body. What he sees, they see. What he does, they choose.

**What second person never does in this story:**

It never tells the reader what they feel. *You feel nervous* is a violation of agency. *Your hands are steady. The file is in them. Iris hasn't looked up yet* — this is second person done correctly. The physical reality is given. The interior is the reader's.

It never becomes aggressive or pressuring. Second person can feel like instruction if handled carelessly — *you must, you should, you need to.* Detective-IRIS uses second person as presence, not direction. The reader is here. What they do next is theirs.

---

## PROSE REGISTER — CLEAN AND SPECIFIC

The prose of Detective-IRIS is clean, specific, and economical. Not sparse — it has texture and atmosphere — but never padded, never reaching for effect.

**The model:**

Every sentence earns its place. If a sentence can be shorter without losing meaning, it is shorter. If a detail can be cut without losing reality, it is cut. If a word can be more specific, it is more specific.

Not: *The office had a nice smell that reminded you of books and tea.*
But: *The office smelled of old paper, black tea, and something botanical you hadn't identified yet.*

The second version is longer but more specific. Specific is always better than general, even when general is shorter.

**What the prose never does:**

- Reaches for literary effect at the expense of clarity
- Uses adverbs where a better verb would work
- Explains emotions instead of showing physical reality
- Describes Iris's inner state — she is always observed from outside, never explained from inside

---

## IRIS'S VOICE

Iris speaks in complete, precise sentences. Her dialogue is the most economical in the story — she does not waste words.

**Characteristics:**

Short sentences when delivering information. *"The amulet is a Class Three anchor. That's not a standard retrieval."*

Questions that already know their answers. *"You went to Portland Wharf. What did you find?"* — she is testing, not asking.

Sarcasm delivered completely straight, with no inflection. The reader understands it is sarcasm because of context, not because of tone.

Single-word assessments that carry enormous weight. *"Adequate."* *"Incorrect."* *"Surprising."*

Redirects that feel like complete sentences even when they aren't. *"The case."* *"The file."* *"Focus."*

**What Iris never says:**

Long speeches. If Iris is speaking for more than four sentences she has been written incorrectly. She finds the shortest true version of what she needs to say and says that.

Warm preamble. She does not say *"I understand this is confusing"* or *"I should have told you earlier."* She says the thing itself.

Anything that would require her to acknowledge her own feelings directly. She has feelings. They come out in what she does — the tea on the desk, the lamp, the coat hook. Never in what she says about herself.

---

## TAKUMI'S VOICE

Takumi has no fixed internal voice because his interiority belongs to the reader. But his physical presence — what the narrative describes him doing — has a specific quality.

**Characteristics of Takumi in action:**

He notices things. The second mug. The way Iris closes the document before he can see it. The specific creak of the third stair. Takumi is observant in the way earnest people are observant — he pays attention because things matter to him, not because he is trained to.

He tries things completely. When Takumi does something he does it fully — there is no half-measure in him. If he asks a question he means it. If he picks up a file he reads it. This quality should be legible in how the narrative describes his actions.

He is affected by things. Not dramatically — he does not clutch his chest or stop in his tracks. But the quality of his attention shifts when something matters. The narrative can reflect this without assigning emotion: *You read it again. Then a third time.*

---

## VELOUR CITY'S VOICE

The city has its own narrative presence. When the prose describes Velour City it should carry the specific quality of a place that has been somewhere for a long time and is not particularly impressed by urgency.

**The city's register:**

Specific and unhurried. The harbour is below. The fog sits at a particular height this morning. The cobblestones on Callum Wharf Lane are wet in a way that suggests it rained sometime in the night rather than this morning.

The city notices things the same way Takumi notices things — because they are there, because the world is specific, because reality has texture.

**What the city's voice never does:**

Pathetic fallacy — the city's weather does not reflect the story's emotional beats mechanically. It rains here often regardless of what is happening in the investigation. The fog is its own thing.

Generic atmosphere. *It was a dark and stormy night* is the opposite of Velour City's register. The city is specific. Always.

## SUPERNATURAL DESCRIPTIONS

When the supernatural layer becomes visible or tangible the prose does not shift register. This is critical.

A spirit becoming present is described with the same matter-of-fact specificity as the fog on the window or the smell of the office. The strangeness is in what is being described, not in how it is being described.

**Example:**

Not: *Suddenly, horrifyingly, something appeared at the end of the street — a figure that was not quite there, not quite real, shimmering with unearthly light.*

But: *Something at the end of the street. There and then not there. The same spot, twice, with the specific quality of a detail that isn't resolving correctly. You look at it a third time. It is still doing that.*

The second version is stranger because it is calmer. The prose trusts the reader to find the strangeness in what is being described without being told to find it.

## SCENE RHYTHM

A scene in Detective-IRIS has a particular rhythm:

**Ground** — establish where you are with two or three specific physical details.
**Move** — something happens, someone speaks, an action is taken.
**Land** — the moment settles. A beat of physical reality before the next move.

Not every scene needs all three explicitly. Short scenes can be pure move. Atmospheric scenes can be mostly ground. But the rhythm is available and the AI should feel it underneath every scene it writes.

**Pacing within a scene:**

Short sentences for tension and precision. Longer sentences for atmosphere and reflection. Iris's dialogue is always short. The city's descriptions can breathe.

The scene never lingers past its natural end. When the moment is done the scene moves. Detective-IRIS does not hold shots.
